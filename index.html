Read each scenario and choose your response.

1. The polished chapter
A doctoral student you have supervised for three years submits a chapter of disquieting accomplishment. A close reading leads you to a near-certain conclusion: she has passed off substantial passages from an obscure monograph as her own thinking, confident no detection algorithm will flag up a source it has never encountered. The plagiarism is systematic enough to rule out negligence, yet not demonstrable enough to hold up before a tribunal lacking your specialist knowledge. Exposing her ends her career. Saying nothing makes you party to a deception that could unravel at any point over the next thirty years.
2. The dominant seminar voice
A student of exceptional ability has crowded out the rest of the seminar — not through aggression, but through a gravitational confidence quieter students cannot match in real time. Participation has levelled off at three or four voices. A pattern is playing out that you recognise from faculty committees: the silent majority's written work is beginning to suffer. The dynamic cuts across the whole cohort. Intervening risks pathologising what makes him exceptional; not intervening normalises a conversational structure some students will carry over into their own careers.
3. The grade appeal
A student lodges a formal appeal you initially dismissed as tactical. Re-reading without marking fatigue, you tease out a counter-argument that is genuinely more sophisticated than you credited. Revising upward is honest — but it throws up a structural problem: four other students received identical marks for comparable work. Whether your original assessment would hold up under external moderation is uncomfortable to consider. Revising opens a cascade; not revising requires you to sustain a judgement you can no longer stand by.
4. The fabricated data
Reviewing a pre-print by the colleague who secured your tenure, you notice a statistical anomaly that is almost certainly deliberate. The research underpins two ongoing clinical trials. Flagging up your concerns means bringing down not only a career but an entire line of inquiry — and triggering a departmental audit from which no one emerges intact. Staying silent is collusion you could not ride out if the fabrication surfaces independently, as it almost certainly will.
5. The viral clip
A deliberately provocative remark — understood as irony by everyone present — has been stripped of context and posted online, where it is playing out as a straightforward expression of the view you were interrogating. The communications office urges you to put out a statement. Any public response risks amplifying the story; a clumsy apology may be read as an admission. The alternative is to sit it out. What you cannot factor in is whether silence will be construed as confirmation.
6. The mature student
A student who spent twenty years in senior risk management cannot convert experiential intelligence into academic currency. He resists every overture of support, reading the offer of guidance as an implicit verdict on his performance. You could map out a tailored remediation path — but without his consent, doing so risks compounding the very humiliation that is holding him back. Leaving him to his own devices — writing him off, in effect — wastes someone who could produce original work by drawing on a practitioner's perspective none of his peers can replicate.
7. The research grant
A leading asset management firm offers to fund a five-year research programme into the long-run performance of actively managed equity funds — precisely the question your department has spent a decade building the data infrastructure to answer. One clause grants the sponsor the right to sit on any findings it deems commercially sensitive for an indefinite period. You have weighed up the alternatives. Accepting means producing work that may never reach publication, or reach it only dressed up to vindicate a fund range the data does not support. Declining means winding up the most significant empirical project of your career. The arrangement is being glossed over in departmental communications as a standard data-access partnership. It is not.
8. The undocumented extension
A student requests a compassionate extension she finds credible but will not document through the welfare process, claiming it would compound her distress. The institutional requirement exists partly to prevent the system being played. Granting an undocumented exception sets a precedent you cannot walk back — word spreads, and you will soon adjudicate claims you cannot verify. Refusing requires you to see through a decision whose practical effects you consider disproportionate, on procedural grounds you privately doubt.
9. The walkout
A third of your students have walked out in protest against a university policy you privately consider indefensible. Those who remained are visibly uncomfortable. The departmental line treats this as a disciplinary matter. You are unwilling to go along with that framing, which would require you to call out behaviour you consider justified. Papering over the genuine grievance through neutral language feels dishonest; staying silent plays into the hands of those claiming the walkout was tacitly approved.
10. The deteriorating candidate
Your most promising doctoral candidate, months from submission, is showing signs you can no longer gloss over: unworkable timelines, erratic communication, analytical capacity breaking down under pressure she refuses to name. Every attempt to open a conversation is brushed off. You suspect she is terrified that acknowledging difficulty will mark her out as unsuitable for the academic career she has planned. Your instinct is to step in. The institution has failed to flesh out its guidance on what that would mean legally or ethically, leaving you to act in a grey area and own the consequences.
