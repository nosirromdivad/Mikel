Activity 1

Read each scenario and choose your response. Your choices are automatically saved.

1. The polished chapter
A doctoral student you have supervised for three years submits a chapter of disquieting accomplishment. A close reading leads you to a near-certain conclusion: she has passed off substantial passages from an obscure monograph as her own thinking, confident no detection algorithm will flag up a source it has never encountered. The plagiarism is systematic enough to rule out negligence, yet not demonstrable enough to hold up before a tribunal lacking your specialist knowledge. Exposing her ends her career. Saying nothing makes you party to a deception that could unravel at any point over the next thirty years.
2. The dominant seminar voice
A student of exceptional ability has crowded out the rest of the seminar. Participation has levelled off at three or four voices. A pattern is playing out that you recognise from faculty committees: the silent majority's written work is beginning to suffer. The dynamic cuts across the whole cohort. Intervening risks pathologising what makes him exceptional; not intervening normalises a conversational structure some students will carry over into their own careers.
3. The grade appeal
A student lodges a formal appeal you initially dismissed as tactical. Re-reading without marking fatigue, you tease out a counter-argument that is genuinely more sophisticated than you credited. Revising upward throws up a structural problem: four other students received identical marks. Whether your original assessment would hold up under external moderation is uncomfortable to consider. Revising opens a cascade; not revising requires you to stand by a judgement you can no longer stand by.
4. The fabricated data
Reviewing a pre-print by the colleague who secured your tenure, you notice a statistical anomaly that is almost certainly deliberate. The research underpins two ongoing clinical trials. Flagging up your concerns means bringing down not only a career but an entire line of inquiry. Staying silent is collusion you could not ride out if the fabrication surfaces independently, as it almost certainly will.
5. The viral clip
A deliberately provocative remark — understood as irony by everyone present — has been stripped of context and posted online. The communications office urges you to put out a statement. Any public response risks amplifying the story; a clumsy apology may be read as an admission. The alternative is to sit it out. What you cannot factor in is whether silence will be construed as confirmation.
6. The mature student
A student who spent twenty years in senior risk management cannot convert experiential intelligence into academic currency. He resists every overture of support. You could map out a tailored remediation path — but without his consent it risks compounding the very humiliation holding him back. Leaving him to his own devices — writing him off, in effect — wastes someone who could produce original work by drawing on a practitioner's perspective none of his peers can replicate.
7. The research grant
A leading asset management firm offers to fund a five-year research programme. One clause grants the sponsor the right to sit on any findings it deems commercially sensitive for an indefinite period. You have weighed up the alternatives. Accepting means producing work that may never reach publication. Declining means winding up the most significant empirical project of your career. The arrangement is being glossed over in departmental communications as a standard data-access partnership. It is not.
8. The undocumented extension
A student requests a compassionate extension she finds credible but will not document through the welfare process, claiming it would compound her distress. Granting an undocumented exception sets a precedent you cannot walk back. Refusing requires you to see through a decision whose practical effects you consider disproportionate.
9. The walkout
A third of your students have walked out in protest against a university policy you privately consider indefensible. The departmental line treats this as a disciplinary matter. You are unwilling to go along with that framing, which would require you to call out behaviour you consider justified. Papering over the genuine grievance feels dishonest; staying silent plays into the hands of those claiming the walkout was tacitly approved.
10. The deteriorating candidate
Your most promising doctoral candidate, months from submission, is showing signs you can no longer gloss over: unworkable timelines, erratic communication, analytical capacity breaking down under pressure she refuses to name. Every attempt to open a conversation is brushed off. Your instinct is to step in. The institution has failed to flesh out its guidance on what that would mean legally or ethically.

Activity 3

Choose the most precise phrasal verb for each sentence.

1. The audit findings had been __________ in the board presentation. No one mentioned the covenant breach.

Think: glossed over = skimmed past without scrutiny. Papered over = actively concealed to create a false appearance of resolution. The findings were mentioned but not examined — they were glossed over.

2. His original forecast didn't __________ once the Q3 data came in.

The forecast itself is being tested. Hold up = remain valid under examination. Bear out = evidence confirms a claim. A theory holds up; data bears out a theory.

3. The restructuring __________ risks that hadn't appeared in the original due diligence.

Threw up = generated unexpectedly (risks emerge on their own). Brought up = a person deliberately raises a topic in conversation.

4. The committee chose to __________ the controversy and wait for the cycle to move on.

Ride out = actively endure and survive a difficult period. Sit out = deliberately abstain from participation. The committee is enduring it, not abstaining.

5. She __________ the criticism in the meeting without responding.

Brushed off = dismissed a comment or person without engaging. Glossed over = skimmed past a topic. You brush off a remark directed at you; you gloss over a subject you're presenting.
Activity 3 answers:

1. glossed over — The findings were skimmed past without scrutiny. "Papered over" implies total concealment.
2. hold up — The forecast itself collapsed under scrutiny. "Bear out" is used when evidence confirms a claim.
3. threw up — Risks emerged unexpectedly from the process. "Brought up" requires a person deliberately raising a topic.
4. ride out — The committee is actively enduring the controversy. "Sit out" means deliberate non-participation.
5. brushed off — She dismissed criticism directed at her. "Glossed over" applies to topics or subjects, not personal remarks.

Where mistakes are buried — and lessons rise from the ashes.

🧠 Make or Do?

MAKE — Use when something is created, produced, or constructed. A result or output comes into existence.
Key collocations: make a decision • make a mistake • make progress • make a profit • make an announcement • make an impression • make a plan • make money
DO — Use when referring to tasks, activities, work, or chores — things you perform or carry out.
Key collocations: do research • do your best • do overtime • do someone a favour • do an exercise • do homework • do damage
• She made an important decision under pressure. (produced a decision)
• He does a lot of research in his own time. (carries out an activity)
• They made significant progress last quarter. (produced a result)
• Can you do me a favour and check this report? (perform a task for someone)

1. We need to ______ a decision before the board meeting.

"Decision" is a result you produce — it collocates with make.

2. Could you ______ me a favour and check this document before I send it?

"A favour" is a task performed for someone — it collocates with do.

3. The company expects to ______ a profit by the end of Q3.

Money and financial results are generated — we make a profit, make money.

4. She always ______ her best when there is pressure on the team.

"Do your best" is a fixed expression — you apply effort, you don't create it.

5. The board will ______ an announcement at the end of the quarter.

An announcement is produced and delivered — "make an announcement" is the standard collocation.

6. He ______ a lot of overtime during the audit season.

Overtime is an activity you carry out — "do overtime" is the fixed expression.

7. It is important to ______ a good impression in the first client meeting.

An impression is something you create in someone else's mind — "make an impression."

8. Can you ______ the calculations again? I think there may be an error in the model.

Calculations are a task you perform — "do the calculations."

9. She ______ a mistake in the analysis but spotted it before submission.

"Make a mistake" is a fixed, very common expression — you produce an error, not perform one.

10. We ______ significant progress on the restructuring plan last month.

"Make progress" = produce advancement. A fixed expression.
Answer Key — Make or Do?

1. make a decision  •  2. do a favour  •  3. make a profit  •  4. do her best  •  5. make an announcement
6. do overtime  •  7. make a good impression  •  8. do the calculations  •  9. make a mistake  •  10. make progress

🧠 The word “so” — four key uses

1. Replacing a clause after: think / hope / believe / suppose / expect / be afraid / imagine
We use so to avoid repeating a whole clause.
“Will the report be ready?” → “I think so.”   “Is there a problem?” → “I’m afraid so.”
2. The negative forms — careful here!
I think so → I don’t think so  (NOT “I think not”)
I hope so → I hope not  (NOT “I don’t hope so”)
I’m afraid so → I’m afraid not
I suppose so → I suppose not / I don’t suppose so
3. Agreement: So / Neither + auxiliary + subject
“She passed the exam.” → “So did I.” (= I also passed)
“He hasn’t replied.” → “Neither has she.” (= She also hasn’t replied)
4. Intensifier
“The results were so much better than expected.”   “It was so difficult to reach a consensus.”

1. “Will the project be approved at the next meeting?”
“I think ______.”

After think, use so to replace the whole clause. “I think it” is not grammatically complete here.

2. “Are you staying for the full conference?”
“I hope ______.”

After hope, use so to replace the clause. “I hope it” is incomplete.

3. “Is there going to be a restructuring?”
“I don’t think ______.”

The negative of “I think so” is “I don’t think so” — we negate the verb, not replace “so” with “not”.

4. “She submitted the proposal before the deadline.”
“______ did I.”

The first sentence is positive, so we use So to agree: “So did I” = I also submitted it.

5. “He hasn’t responded to the proposal yet.”
“______ has my manager, unfortunately.”

The first sentence is negative (hasn’t), so we use Neither: “Neither has my manager” = My manager also hasn’t responded.

6. “Will there be redundancies in the new structure?”
“I’m afraid ______.”

After be afraid, use so to replace the positive clause. “I’m afraid so” = I’m sorry to confirm yes, there will be.

7. The final results were ______ much stronger than the preliminary data suggested.

Before much, only so works naturally as an intensifier. “So much stronger” is the emphatic, natural form.

8. “Did the committee approve the revised budget?”
“I believe ______.”

After believe, use so to replace the clause — same pattern as think and hope.

9. “She doesn’t want to present at the external conference.”
“______ does he, apparently.”

The first sentence is negative (doesn’t), so we use Neither: “Neither does he” = He also doesn’t want to.

10. “The deadline has been moved to next Friday.”
“I suppose ______. It gives us more time at least.”

After suppose, use so to replace the clause. “I suppose so” signals reluctant acceptance.
Answer Key — The word “so”

1. so • 2. so • 3. so (I don’t think so) • 4. So did I • 5. Neither has my manager
6. so • 7. so (intensifier before “much”) • 8. so • 9. Neither does he • 10. so
